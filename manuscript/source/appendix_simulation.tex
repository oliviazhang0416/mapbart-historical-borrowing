\section{Simulation design and complete results}\label{sec:E}
\subsection{Covariates, selection and scenario definitions}\label{sec:E.1}
Each setting has ten observed covariates. Here $X_j$ denotes scalar predictor $j$, whereas the bold $\mathbf{X}_a$ in the model denotes the covariate collection for group $a$. For a profile $x$, $x_j$ denotes coordinate $j$. Before source selection, the ordinary covariates are independent normals with unit variance and mean vector
\begin{equation}
(1,-1,0.5,-1,2,-2,2,-3,1,0).
\end{equation}
With probability 0.15, $X_1$ is instead drawn from an equally weighted mixture of $\N(-3,0.5^2)$ and $\N(3,0.5^2)$. Separate trial and historical candidate pools are generated. In Sc1, Sc4 and Sc5, source eligibility is independent of covariates. In Sc2, the trial-eligibility probability is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:select}
\operatorname{logit}^{-1}\left\{\frac{b_D(X_5+X_6)-q}{t_D}\right\},
\end{equation}
where $q$ is the empirical quantile of the pool's selection score at the intended trial fraction. Two-arm studies use $b_D=-1.20,t_D=0.5$; single-arm studies use $b_D=-0.55,t_D=1$. Thus the single-arm and two-arm selection strengths differ. Exact numbers of eligible trial and historical observations are then sampled from their respective pools. In two-arm trials, treatment is assigned independently with the probability in main Table 1.

Sc3 is included only in the two-arm studies. Latent $U_5,U_6$ replace $X_5,X_6$ in both the outcome function and selection score. They are generated from the corresponding normal distributions. The observed proxies are $X_j=\rho U_j^{\mathrm{std}}+\sqrt{1-\rho^2}\,e_j$, where $U_j^{\mathrm{std}}$ is standardized within its candidate pool and $e_j\sim\N(0,1)$ independently. We use $\rho=-0.5,0,0.5$. Conditioning on these proxies generally does not remove source differences induced by latent-variable selection.

Sc4 adds $\delta\in\{1,2\}$ to every historical Gaussian outcome or latent log event time. Sc5 adds $\delta$ only outside a compatible region: $X_5>2$ with $\delta\in\{0.5,1,2\}$, or $X_7\leq2$ with $\delta\in\{1,2\}$. The true trial-minus-historical discrepancy is zero inside that region and $-\delta$ outside.

\subsection{Outcome functions and censoring}\label{sec:E.2}
The common nonlinear mean function, before a treatment or historical shift, is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dgp}
\begin{aligned}
F(x)\coloneqq {}&b_0+\beta_1x_1^2+\beta_2\exp\{-(x_2+1)^2/2\}
+\beta_3|x_3-0.5|+\beta_4|x_4+1|\\
&+\beta_5\tanh(x_5-2)+\beta_6\tanh(x_6+2)\\
&+2\left\{\frac{(x_5-2)^2}{1+((x_5-2)/1.5)^2}
+\frac{(x_6+2)^2}{1+((x_6+2)/1.5)^2}\right\}
+\sum_{j=7}^{10}\beta_jx_j,
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
with $\beta=(-0.50,-0.75,-0.50,-0.50,-1.35,-0.80,-0.01,-0.01,-0.01,-0.01)$. In Sc3, substitute the latent variables in the fifth and sixth positions. For Gaussian outcomes add independent normal noise and a treatment effect of 1 for treated trial participants. For survival outcomes exponentiate the noisy log-time response, with treatment effect $\log(1.4)$. The effect is constant in the reported settings; no additional treatment-by-covariate interaction is generated.

Survival observations are $\min(T,C_d,C_a)$ with event indicator $\ind(T\leq C_d,T\leq C_a)$. Two-arm administrative follow-up is generated as 3 minus a uniform entry time on $(0,1)$; single-arm follow-up is 5.5 minus uniform entry on $(0,0.5)$. Historical shifts are applied to latent event times before evaluating censoring. The historical candidate pool is regenerated independently for each replicate.

\subsection{Truth and target population}\label{sec:E.3}
For each dataset, true conditional means and control/treatment survival distributions are evaluated at that dataset's trial profiles. The Gaussian target is their average treatment-minus-control difference, which equals 1 under the constant-effect design. The survival primary target is the ratio of medians of the two trial-standardized population curves, equal to 1.4 under the generated common log-time shift. The RMST target uses the corresponding restricted integrals and varies with the profiles and restriction horizon. Squared errors are computed against the relevant dataset-specific truth before aggregation.

The hierarchical comparator uses source-specific regression coefficients with a common estimated hypermean. Intercept and predictor-specific discrepancy-variance components have $\IG(3/2,3s_\tau^2/2)$ priors, where $s_\tau^2$ is the scale listed in Table S3; it is not a probability or historical sample-size fraction. Hypermeans have diffuse normal priors. This comparator pools regression parameters through their prior rather than specifying a nonlinear discrepancy surface.

The MAP comparator uses the historical sample mean and its standard error in a study-level normal model, a normal location prior with standard deviation 10, and an inverse-gamma prior $\IG(3/2,3a/2)$ for its heterogeneity parameter, with $a$ selected by the MAP prior ESS procedure. Its predictive prior is approximated by a normal mixture and updated using the trial-control sample mean and standard error. This study-level comparator does not use a BART outcome model. Its prior ESS procedure is distinct from the lrcBART definition. PSCL follows the propensity-score-integrated construction described by \citet{Chen2020PSCL}.

There are 17 method/configuration combinations per Gaussian two-arm setting and 13 per survival two-arm setting. Each reported single-arm setting has eight combinations. All reported configurations have 100 completed replicates.

\subsection{Performance measures and Monte Carlo uncertainty}\label{sec:E.5}
Let $\psi$ denote the estimand being evaluated: the Gaussian mean contrast, survival median ratio or RMST ratio. For point estimates $\widehat\psi_r$ and dataset-specific truths $\psi_r$, write $e_r\coloneqq \widehat\psi_r-\psi_r$ over $R=100$ replicates. We calculate
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\mathrm{Bias}}\coloneqq R^{-1}\sum_r e_r,\qquad
\widehat{\mathrm{RMSE}}\coloneqq \sqrt{R^{-1}\sum_r e_r^2}.
\end{equation}
Gaussian point estimates use posterior means and survival ratios use posterior medians, with the corresponding point estimates used for likelihood-based methods. Coverage is the proportion of reported 95\% intervals containing truth. The uncertainty column reports the mean posterior SD for Bayesian methods and the mean standard error for PSCL; interval width is averaged within each setting. These summaries differ from the empirical SD of point estimates across datasets.

For Bayesian benefit decisions, empirical power is the proportion satisfying posterior probability above 0.95 for an effect greater than 0.5 (Gaussian) or a ratio greater than 1 (survival). The PSCL decision instead requires its lower confidence limit to exceed the Gaussian threshold. These are different decision rules. Type I error was not evaluated, and the rules were not calibrated to a common demonstrated Type I error rate.

Monte Carlo standard errors are
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(\widehat{\mathrm{Bias}})=\frac{s_e}{\sqrt R},\qquad
\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(\widehat{\mathrm{RMSE}})\simeq
\frac{s_{e^2}}{2\sqrt R\,\widehat{\mathrm{RMSE}}},\qquad
\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(\widehat p)=\sqrt{\widehat p(1-\widehat p)/R}.
\end{equation}
Here $s_e$ and $s_{e^2}$ are the sample standard deviations of the replicate errors and squared errors, and $\widehat p$ is the corresponding empirical coverage or power proportion. The RMSE formula is a delta-method approximation. A plug-in proportion standard error of zero at an observed proportion of zero or one does not establish absence of uncertainty; exact binomial intervals would still have nonzero width. These errors describe each row, not a paired between-method contrast. Comparisons should account for the common datasets if formal uncertainty in performance differences is required.
